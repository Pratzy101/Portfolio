Flagship KPMG · Steel manufacturing client · 2022

GAP Reporting Tool

A compliance gap register for a Luxembourg steel manufacturer, taken from scattered spreadsheets to a single system of record used by more than a hundred people.

Role
Analyst — design, build, data layer, UAT
Client
Steel manufacturer, Luxembourg
Delivery
Agile, KPMG GRCS
Stack
Power Apps, Power Automate, Power BI, Figma
Context

KPMG's Governance, Risk & Compliance practice ran the engagement. A compliance gap, in this setting, is any place where a documented process and the process actually being followed have come apart — and every one of them has to be recorded, assigned to somebody, worked, and closed, with evidence that it happened.

The client had the discipline. What they did not have was anywhere for it to live.

Problem

Gaps were tracked across scattered spreadsheets. That produced three failures that compounded each other:

  • No single owner. A gap could sit in two workbooks with two different people each assuming the other had it.
  • No audit trail. Spreadsheets record the current state, not how it got there. In a compliance context that is the part that matters — an auditor asks when a gap was raised, who accepted it, and what changed since.
  • No reliable rollup. Management reporting meant someone manually reconciling workbooks, so the number was always slightly wrong and always late.


The spreadsheets were not the root problem. The absence of a system of record was.

Constraints

The shape of the solution was largely set before any design work started:

  • Microsoft-only estate. The client was already on Microsoft 365, which made Power Platform the path of least resistance for both licensing and IT approval.
  • Had to survive handover. Whatever got built would be maintained by the client after the engagement closed, by people who are not developers. That ruled out anything needing a build pipeline.
  • Audit trail was non-negotiable. Not a feature request — the reason the tool existed.
Architecture
Runtime architecture. Figma sat upstream of all of this as the design step, not a running component.

Five tiers, each one dependent only on the tier below it. The interface holds no logic and the flows hold no state — everything durable lives in the data layer, which is what makes the audit trail trustworthy rather than best-effort.

The design decision worth defending is the separate audit table. It would have been faster to timestamp columns on the gap record itself. But that stores only the latest change, and the question an auditor asks is about the sequence. Writing every state transition as its own row costs storage and buys the ability to answer that question.

The data layer was implemented using SharePoint Lists.

What I built

Design

Wireframed the interface in Figma before building anything. On a Power Apps project this is skippable and usually skipped — you can drag controls onto a canvas and have something running in an hour. Doing the design step first meant the disagreements about what a gap record should contain happened over a wireframe rather than over a half-built app.

Build

Built the canvas app, the Power Automate flows behind it, and the database layer underneath. Delivered on an Agile cadence with the client seeing working software each iteration rather than a status deck.

Validation

Led UAT with the people who would actually use it. This is where the design assumptions got tested against how compliance leads really work, and where the tool stopped being what I thought they needed.

Handover

Deployed as the single system of record and handed to the client to run.

Outcome
  • Deployed to 100+ internal users as the single system of record for gap reporting.
  • Every state change captured, so the audit question became a query instead of an archaeology exercise.
  • Management reporting fed from one source rather than a manual reconciliation.


Power Platform made it possible to ship fast and leave the client something they could maintain without us. That was the right trade-off for the constraints.

What I'd do differently

Power Platform got this shipped fast and left the client something they could maintain without us. Those were the right trade-offs for the constraints. But two things I would approach differently now:

  • No automated tests. Every regression was caught by a human clicking through the app. That is fine for one release and it does not scale — the client inherited a tool where any change carries risk they have no way to measure.
  • Solution packaging was an afterthought. Environments and managed solutions were something to sort out at deployment rather than a decision made at the start, which made moving between dev and production more manual than it needed to be.


The broader lesson is that low-code shifts effort rather than removing it. It buys speed at the front and charges for it in lifecycle management, and knowing which of those two you actually need is the real decision.
